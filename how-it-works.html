02The mathematical problem

The word cheapest deserves some attention. What if we want to spend as little as possible on just one can of beer, or we need to buy a lot, so it would be better to measure the price per liter? These are different questions, but both belong to discrete minimization problems.

Cities and sets

We consider two cities: Garching and Munich.

The symbol c represents whichever city we are currently examining.

We calculate the results separately because supermarket prices may differ between cities and individual branches. Lidl generally follows a uniform pricing policy, while each observation in our database remains associated with its recorded location.

For a given city c, let

Ic={eligible Radler offers in city c}

For the first problem, we introduce a smaller set:

Ic∗⊆Ic

It contains offers where one bottle or can can be purchased individually, with a known deposit and a minimum purchase quantity of one.

Consider three offers:

OfferIcIc∗
One 0.5 L bottle at LidlYesYes
Six 0.5 L bottles at LidlYes—
One 0.5 L can at REWEYesYes

Parameters

Each offer is identified by an index i.

SymbolMeaning
αiPackage price excluding deposit (€)
βiRefundable deposit, Pfand (€)
γiNumber of containers in a package
δiVolume per container (liters)
εiIndividual purchase availability: 1 if available, 0 otherwise

The smaller set can now be written as

Ic∗={i∈Ic:εi=1,γi=1}

with the additional requirements of a known deposit and a minimum purchase quantity of one.

Problem 1 — Minimum price per bottle

Our first objective is the smallest amount payable for one bottle or can, including Pfand.

mini∈Ic∗(αi+βi)

Problem 2 — Minimum price per liter

Our second objective is the lowest beverage price per liter. Here, individual bottles and multipacks compete together.

mini∈Icαiγi⁢δi

The refundable deposit is excluded from this comparison.

An example

On 13 October 2026, I found Perlenbacher Radler at Lidl on Schleißheimer Straße.

One 0.5 L bottle cost €0.45, plus €0.25 Pfand.

0.45+0.25=0.70 €

A six-pack cost €2.69 for three liters:

2.696·0.5≈0.897 €/L

The individual bottle costs €0.90/L.

A difference of roughly a third of a cent per liter.

An impressively small reward for such a respectable mathematical formulation.

03The algorithm

Imagine a row of price tags. We remember the first eligible price. Then we examine the next one. Whenever we find something cheaper, we remember the new price instead. After examining the last offer, we have our minimum. This is called Linear Minimum Search. We apply it twice for each city: once for the cheapest individual purchase and once for the lowest price per liter. Before comparing offers, we select records satisfying the conditions of our model: alcoholic Radler, verified prices, publicly available discounts, and applicable offer dates. If two prices are equal, the more recent observation wins. For n offers, the search takes O(n) time.

04The data

Our price database combines three sources.

Shelf observations. I photograph prices in supermarkets. Visitors can contribute observations through the reporting form. Each submission is reviewed manually before entering the verified data.

Official online prices. Retailer websites provide additional reference prices.

Automated collection. A Python script retrieves publicly available Kaufland offers for Munich branches. GitHub Actions schedules the process daily.

Each record includes the supermarket, location, product, package size, price, deposit, and observation date. Prices older than 14 days are marked as potentially outdated. Their dates remain visible, allowing readers to judge how recent an observation is.

05The implementation

The architecture follows the mathematical problem.

Collect → Validate → Compare → Display

Prices enter data.json through manual observations and automated collection.

The implementation uses:

  • HTML and CSS for the interface.
  • JavaScript for eligibility checks and minimum search.
  • JSON for the price records.
  • Python 3 and its standard libraries for data collection and validation.
  • GitHub Actions for scheduled updates.
  • GitHub Pages for hosting.
  • FormSubmit for collecting visitor observations.

The code is public. The calculations can be inspected, reproduced, and improved.

06Where could this lead?

Our current model has two objectives, each solved independently. Now imagine adding the journey to the supermarket. A cheaper bottle might require a longer trip. Travel costs introduce another consideration. A fixed budget introduces a constraint. Buying drinks for a party introduces quantities and combinations of packages. Soon, an ordinary shopping decision becomes a richer optimization problem. We could study multiple objectives, integer programming, or the relationship between travel cost and product price. That is what I enjoy about mathematics.

The world offers questions everywhere. We only have to notice them.
